Amino Acids and Proteins

Amino acids are the fundamental building blocks of proteins and play a vital role in all living organisms. They are organic compounds containing both an amino group (-NH₂) and a carboxyl group (-COOH) attached to the same carbon atom known as the alpha carbon. The general formula of an amino acid is NH₂-CH(R)-COOH, where R represents the side chain that differs from one amino acid to another. There are about 20 common amino acids that participate in protein synthesis. Amino acids are amphoteric in nature, meaning they can act as both acids and bases. In aqueous solution, they exist as zwitterions, containing both positive and negative charges within the same molecule. Based on nutritional requirements, amino acids are classified into essential and non-essential amino acids. Essential amino acids such as lysine, methionine, leucine, isoleucine, valine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, and threonine cannot be synthesized by the human body and must be obtained from food. Non-essential amino acids can be synthesized by the body.

Amino acids combine through peptide bonds to form proteins. A peptide bond is formed when the carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of another amino acid with the elimination of a water molecule. Two amino acids form a dipeptide, three form a tripeptide, and many amino acids linked together form a polypeptide chain. Proteins are high molecular weight biological macromolecules composed of one or more polypeptide chains. They are essential components of cells and tissues and perform numerous biological functions.

Proteins are classified based on shape and composition. Based on shape, proteins are divided into fibrous and globular proteins. Fibrous proteins are long, thread-like, insoluble in water, and mainly provide structural support. Examples include keratin and collagen. Globular proteins are compact, spherical, water-soluble, and perform metabolic functions. Examples include hemoglobin, insulin, and enzymes. Based on composition, proteins are classified as simple proteins, conjugated proteins, and derived proteins. Simple proteins yield only amino acids upon hydrolysis, while conjugated proteins contain a non-protein prosthetic group such as carbohydrates, lipids, or metal ions.

Proteins possess four levels of structural organization. The primary structure refers to the specific sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide chain. The secondary structure involves local folding into alpha-helices and beta-pleated sheets stabilized by hydrogen bonds. The tertiary structure represents the three-dimensional arrangement of a single polypeptide chain maintained by hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, hydrophobic forces, and disulfide bonds. The quaternary structure is formed when two or more polypeptide chains associate to produce a functional protein, as seen in hemoglobin.

Proteins perform various functions in living organisms. They act as enzymes that catalyze biochemical reactions, hormones that regulate physiological processes, antibodies that provide immunity, transport molecules such as hemoglobin that carry oxygen, and structural components such as collagen and keratin. Proteins are also involved in muscle contraction, cell signaling, growth, tissue repair, and maintenance of body functions. Denaturation is the process in which proteins lose their natural structure and biological activity due to heat, acids, alkalis, or chemicals without breaking peptide bonds. Thus, amino acids and proteins are indispensable biomolecules that are essential for life, growth, metabolism, and numerous industrial, medical, and biotechnological applications.
